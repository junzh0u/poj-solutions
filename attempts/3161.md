POJ 3161, Numerical Integration, asks for four decimal places for convergent integrals of parsed expressions on intervals of length at most ten. Allowed functions are sin, cos, tan, log, exp, asin, acos, atan, and abs. Expressions also contain C floating constants, x, parentheses, unary signs, and binary + - * / ^. Power is right associative and has precedence above multiplication/division; the statement otherwise preserves C precedence, so unary signs bind above power. Input ends at EOF. Singularities can occur internally or at endpoints, need not be removable, and are guaranteed isolated and nonoscillatory in a neighborhood where the function blows up. Elsewhere arbitrarily oscillatory functions are permitted.

One submission was made under model gpt-6.1-sol: run 25225562, C++, 4054B, Wrong Answer. No accepted archive was written.

The submitted algorithm compiled each expression into postfix operations using recursive descent. It used the standard open 15-point Kronrod/7-point Gauss embedded quadrature rule, initially dividing each integration interval into 64 equal parts. Within a part it compared the two estimates, recursively halved intervals, and divided an absolute error budget of 1e-8 between children. The maximum recursion depth was 48, with an evaluation ceiling of two million. Since neither rule samples endpoints, removable and logarithmic endpoint singularities do not require evaluating the undefined endpoint. Outputs with magnitude below 0.00005 were normalized to positive zero.

Local testing exposed slow convergence on x^-0.9 over [0,1]. An extrapolation patch, included in the submission, computed composite Kronrod estimates with 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 panels after twenty recursive halvings. Three overlapping Aitken delta-squared extrapolates were compared, and a stable pair accepted. This repaired the lower-endpoint pure-power tests, including x^-0.99, but was not a generally reliable singularity method.

The sample passed exactly. A further 509 analytic cases passed: 250 random sine integrals with frequencies up to 2000, 250 random absolute-value integrals, five lower-endpoint powers with exponents from -0.1 through -0.9, plus log(x), a frequency-100000 sine, unary precedence, and power associativity. The associativity test 2^3^2 over [0,1] expects 512; a deliberately broken parser returned 8, so that test detects the targeted mutation. These successes do not certify arbitrary singularities.

After Wrong Answer, two concrete deficiencies were found. First, 1/(1-x)^0.9 over [0,1] returned 9.3420 rather than 10.0000: extrapolating so close to the upper endpoint amplifies cancellation in evaluating 1-x. Moving the extrapolation to only four recursive halvings fixed this case locally, but that change was not submitted. Second, the original input reader assumed the expression was one whitespace-delimited token. A later local revision instead took the last two tokens of each line as bounds and concatenated all earlier tokens, permitting spaces inside the expression; this was not submitted either.

The unresolved separating case is:

    1/abs(x-0.3)^0.9 0 1

Its exact integral is (0.3^0.1 + 0.7^0.1)/0.1, approximately 18.515292456850307, while both versions returned 18.5241. The singularity is not aligned with dyadic interval boundaries; ordinary subdivision reaches floating-point resolution before collecting its substantial remaining mass. Endpoint-only extrapolation does not repair this. A retry needs a robust improper-integral scheme that can locate or extrapolate interior singularities, such as a carefully implemented globally adaptive quadrature with extrapolation, and must validate against analytic strong singularities at non-dyadic points. Merely increasing depth or loosening the acceptance test is not justified.
